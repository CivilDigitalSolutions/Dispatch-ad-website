How to quote for a job and take a deposit up front

A quote sitting unanswered in an inbox is not a job. Here is how to price the work, force a decision, and have the deposit in the bank before you order materials.

Last updated: August 2026

A quote is a decision point, not a document

Most quotes that go cold do not go cold because the price was wrong. They go cold because nothing ever forced a decision. You priced the job, you emailed a PDF, and then the work moved into the worst category a job can occupy: waiting on someone else to remember you exist.

The fix is to make accepting easy and to attach a small, reasonable commitment to it. A customer who has paid a deposit has decided. A customer who said “yeah, that sounds fine” on the doorstep has not.

What a quote needs to contain

You do not need a legal document. You need enough detail that nobody can honestly claim later that they thought something else was included.

  • The work, in plain language. What you are actually going to do, described the way you would describe it to the customer’s face.
  • What is not included. The single most valuable line in any quote. Making good, decorating, scaffolding, disposal, parking — whatever you are not doing, say so.
  • Materials. Itemised or stated as a lump sum, but be clear which.
  • The total, and whether it includes VAT. Ambiguity here costs you 20% of an argument you will probably lose.
  • The deposit. The amount, and what it covers.
  • Rough timing, and how long the price holds. Material prices move. An open-ended quote is one you will regret in four months.

How much deposit to ask for

There is no industry rule, and anyone who tells you there is one is guessing. The useful test is this: if this customer changed their mind the day after accepting, what would I be out of pocket?

For a job where you are ordering a specific boiler, a made-to-measure unit or anything non-returnable, the deposit should cover that order. For a labour-heavy job using generic materials it can be much smaller — there you are asking for commitment, not funding. In practice most domestic trades land somewhere between 20% and 50%, but treat that as a description of what people do, not a target to hit.

Two things matter more than the percentage. Ask for the same proportion consistently, so you are not renegotiating it on every job. And put it in the quote, so the customer agrees to it at the same moment they agree to the price.

On consumer rights: customers who agree a contract at their own home, or at a distance, have cancellation rights that do not apply between businesses. Set out your cancellation and refund terms in the quote rather than leaving them to be discovered later, and take proper advice if you routinely take large deposits from domestic customers.

Getting an answer without the phone tag

The gap between sending a quote and hearing back is where jobs die. Every step you add — print it, sign it, scan it, post it — is another chance for the customer to put it on the pile.

What works is a quote they can read on their phone and accept or decline in one tap, with the deposit payable in the same place. No account to create, no app to install, no calling you back during working hours when they are also at work.

What should happen after acceptance

An accepted quote should become a scheduled job the same day, while the customer is still warm. That means booking the visit, telling the customer when you are coming, and making sure whoever does the work can see what was actually quoted rather than a summary of it.

This is where quoting connects to everything else. The accepted quote, the deposit invoice, the photos from the original fault report and the final invoice all belong to the same job — not to four different systems. If your quotes live in email, your schedule lives in a diary and your invoices live in a spreadsheet, somebody spends every Friday evening reconciling the three.

Once the work is booked, job scheduling for a small trades team covers keeping the week straight, and chasing an unpaid invoice covers the part nobody enjoys.

Three mistakes worth avoiding

  1. Quoting verbally. A number said out loud in a hallway is not a quote, and you will remember it differently from the customer.
  2. Rounding to a suspiciously neat figure. A quote of exactly £2,000 reads as a guess. £1,940 reads as a calculation, because it is one.
  3. Not following up. One polite follow-up a few days later rescues a meaningful number of quotes that would otherwise expire in silence. Set the reminder the moment you send it.

Questions

Quoting & deposits FAQ

How much deposit should a tradesperson ask for?

There is no fixed rule. Most trades set the deposit to cover materials and any non-refundable orders, which on a typical domestic job lands somewhere between 20% and 50% of the quote. The test is simple: if the customer walked away the day after accepting, would you be out of pocket? The deposit should cover that gap and no more.

Can I ask a domestic customer for a deposit?

Yes, provided the deposit is clearly stated in the quote the customer accepts, so they agree to it before any money changes hands. Consumer customers also have cancellation rights on contracts agreed at home or at a distance, so make your cancellation and refund terms explicit in the quote rather than leaving them assumed.

What should a quote include?

A description of the work in plain language, what is and is not included, materials, the total price and whether it includes VAT, the deposit amount, roughly when you can start, and how long the price holds. Anything you leave vague is something you will end up arguing about later.
